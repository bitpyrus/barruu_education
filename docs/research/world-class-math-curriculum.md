# Build Barruu's maths on a coherent international spine

Barruu should not copy one national syllabus. It should build one **coherent, prerequisite-ordered skill graph that covers the union of what the top systems teach**, then deliver it as 27 short, topic-sized courses in seven strands, running from rational numbers (grade 6) to linear algebra and discrete maths (university year 1). The structure should copy what Singapore and Japan get right: a few strands revisited with more depth each year, early formal algebra and geometry, and problem solving as the organising goal. The *scope* should copy England, Singapore and Cambridge, which teach 2D vectors, the sine and cosine rules, set notation, iteration and even first differentiation by age 15–16, while US CCSS marks these "(+)" optional or leaves them out. The first thing to build is the **grade 8–9 "hinge"**: linear relationships and functions, plus equations taught through a balance scale and algebra tiles. It is the most heavily used prerequisite in every system, Barruu's existing function explorer already fits it, and covariational reasoning (how two quantities change together) is the best predictor of calculus readiness. The interactives that matter most are a **number line**, a **balance scale / algebra-tiles board**, a **linked table–graph–equation function lab with a motion "drive the graph" mode**, a **transformation and construction board**, and a **probability simulator**. Each of these serves five or more courses. Every lesson should follow evidence that is well replicated but modest in size: concreteness fading, worked examples that fade adaptively, error analysis, interleaved and spaced review, and distractors mapped to named misconceptions. Realistic gains are **about 0.2–0.4 SD on standardized measures**, not Bloom's "2 sigma". Placement should keep the existing Rasch/EAP stage for a coarse band, then run a knowledge-space search over the skill graph to find the learner's "ready to learn" fringe.

## Top systems teach fewer topics, earlier, in strict prerequisite order

The same East Asian group leads both major international assessments. In TIMSS 2023 grade 8, **Singapore scored 605, Chinese Taipei 602, Korea 596 and Japan 595**, against an international average of 478, with England at 525 and the United States at 488 ([CUHK TIMSS 2023 annex](https://www.cpr.cuhk.edu.hk/wp-content/uploads/newscentre/pressrelease/Annex-English_TIMSS-2023.pdf)). In PISA 2022, Singapore scored **575**, significantly above every other participant; the OECD mean was 472 and had fallen 15 points since 2018 ([OECD PISA 2022 Vol. I](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-i_53f23881-en/full-report/component-9.html)). Reputation does not protect a system: Finland has fallen **64 points from its 2006 peak** to 484 ([University of Jyväskylä](https://www.jyu.fi/en/news/pisa-2022-performance-fell-both-in-finland-and-in-nearly-all-other-oecd-countries)).

The best-supported curricular explanation is old, but nothing since has overturned it. Schmidt's TIMSS analyses found that "student performance is directly related to the nature of the curricular expectations", and he named three properties of high-performing curricula: **focus** (few enough topics that teachers spend "months, not days" on each), **rigor** (algebra and geometry in the middle grades) and **coherence** (sequencing that "follows the structure of the discipline"). He treats coherence as the most important ([Schmidt 2008, American Educator](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/schmidt.pdf)). PISA 2012 opportunity-to-learn analyses found that **exposure to formal mathematics** was related to performance in every country ([Schmidt & Burroughs 2015](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/ae_spring2015schmidt_burroughs.pdf)). Both findings are correlational. Current national documents put the same principles into practice. Singapore's 12-year curriculum is explicitly hierarchical ("higher concepts and skills are built upon foundational ones and learned in sequence") and spiral, with strands revisited "to achieve greater depth" and problem solving at the centre of its framework ([TIMSS 2023 Encyclopedia: Singapore](https://timss2023.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Singapore.pdf)). Japan runs four lower-secondary domains (Numbers and Algebraic Expressions, Geometric Figures, Functions, Making Use of Data). In grade 8 its students discover properties of figures "through activities such as observing, manipulating, and experimenting" and then prove them from congruence conditions ([TIMSS 2023 Encyclopedia: Japan](https://timss2023.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Japan.pdf)). "Discover by manipulating, then verify" is exactly a Barruu lesson arc.

On scope, the top non-US systems reach by age 16 roughly what CCSS reaches by the end of Algebra II. GCSE Higher expects completing the square, inverse and composite functions, **gradients and areas under graphs as instantaneous rates**, numerical iteration, algebraic proof and proofs of the circle theorems ([DfE GCSE subject content](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7cb5b040f0b6629523b52c/GCSE_mathematics_subject_content_and_assessment_objectives.pdf)). Cambridge IGCSE Extended includes sets, 2D vectors, exact trig values, the sine and cosine rules and **differentiation (E2.12)** for 14–16-year-olds ([Cambridge 0580 2025–2027](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/662466-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf)). In CCSS, by contrast, vectors and matrices are almost entirely "(+)", the sine and cosine rules are "(+)" (G-SRT.10–11), and there is no calculus at all ([CCSS-M](https://learning.ccsso.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf)). Even AP Precalculus puts vectors, matrices, parametrics and conics in an **unassessed Unit 4** (0% of the exam) ([AP Precalculus Course at a Glance](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-precalculus-course-at-a-glance.pdf)). In the final two years every non-US specialist track (A-level, Singapore H2, IB AA/AI) makes calculus, vectors and statistical inference with named distributions compulsory together ([DfE A-level content](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7f273a40f0b62305b85670/GCE_AS_and_A_level_subject_content_for_mathematics_with_appendices.pdf); [Singapore H2 9758](https://www3.ntu.edu.sg/oad2/EE%20Questions/A_Maths_syllabus_2020.pdf)). CCSS has real strengths the others lack: **statistics from grade 6** (variability, sampling, bivariate data, simulation-based inference) and modelling as a cross-cutting category. Barruu should keep both.

So the design stance is: **CCSS-style statistics and modelling, England/Singapore scope, Singapore/Japan coherence.** The graph's ordering of skills follows mathematical structure. Each learner's pace follows placement. Any one syllabus is just a *view* of the graph defined by alignment tags (see the standards section). This also fits the platforms. Brilliant splits Algebra I into small topic courses (Linear Equations, Linear Relationships, Exponents and Radicals, Visual Algebra) and runs geometry, probability/data and logic tracks alongside the algebra spine ([Brilliant help](https://brilliant.org/help/courses-and-curriculum/what-math-does-brilliant-cover/)). AoPS treats counting, probability and number theory as optional side branches ([AoPS recommendations](https://artofproblemsolving.com/store/recommendations.php)). Both behave like a graph, not a single ladder.

## Seven strands and 27 topic-sized courses form the sequence

The prerequisite chain is the same in every framework studied. Proportional reasoning leads to linear relationships, which lead to the function concept, then to families of functions distinguished by how they change, then to inverses (logarithms), then circular functions, then calculus. Geometry runs in parallel (angles and similarity, then Pythagoras, then right-triangle and non-right trigonometry, then vectors, then matrices as transformations). The data strand runs from description to probability, distributions and inference. The strands meet at a few junctions: **slope** (similar triangles), **trigonometric functions** (unit circle), **linear fit** (bivariate data needs linear functions), and **matrices** (they are geometric transformations). CCSS grade 8 is where functions, irrationals, exponents, linear systems, transformations and Pythagoras all converge ([CCSS-M](https://learning.ccsso.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/ADA-Compliant-Math-Standards.pdf)), and that is why it is the hinge.

The recommended strands are **N** Number and proportion, **A** Algebra and functions (the spine), **G** Geometry, trigonometry and vectors, **D** Data, probability and statistics, **C** Calculus, **P** Logic, proof and discrete maths, and **L** Linear algebra. Courses are kept to topic size (about 3–6 chapters of 3–5 short lessons each), which matches Brilliant's granularity and short self-paced sessions. Each band also gets a Khan-style **"Get ready" bridge**: a short refresher of only the prerequisite skills, generated from the graph, so a learner who places slightly low does not repeat a whole course ([Khan student guide](https://emails.khanacademy.org/media/pdf/sat-student-guide.pdf)).

Three deliberate choices stand out. **V5 is a precalculus bridge on rate of change** because the readiness evidence points there. Bressoud's summary of the MAA national calculus study shows many students who completed high-school calculus being placed back into precalculus or college algebra ([Bressoud 2015](https://www.math.stonybrook.edu/~bishop/classes/math598.F24/Bressoud-InsightsMAANational-2015.pdf)). Taking college precalculus did not raise later calculus grades ([Sonnert & Sadler, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10556619)). The validated readiness instruments are built around covariation: 18 of the CCR's 25 items assess or rely on it ([CCR, arXiv 1010.2719](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1010.2719)). More procedural algebra will not fix that gap. Covariation lessons will. Second, **mechanics stays in physics**. England makes it compulsory in A-level maths, but Cambridge 9709 makes it optional and IB and CCSS omit it ([Cambridge 9709](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/programmes-and-qualifications/cambridge-international-as-and-a-level-mathematics-9709/)). Barruu already has physics labs, so mechanics can be cross-tagged rather than duplicated. Third, Barruu should offer a **statistics path alongside calculus**, as the Dana Center's Launch Years work (27 states by October 2025) advocates ([Dana Center](https://utdanacenter.org/launchyears)). It should also follow the new AP Statistics order (five units from Fall 2026, with regression moved to the end) ([AP Statistics CED 2026](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-statistics-course-and-exam-description.pdf)).

## Every course ships with a misconception catalogue wired into distractors

The EEF's review of KS2/KS3 maths says misconceptions should be uncovered, not side-stepped, and warns that pupils defend misconceptions rooted in sound but limited ideas, such as "multiplication makes bigger" or 1/8 + 1/2 = 2/10 ([EEF guidance](https://dera.ioe.ac.uk/30457/1/KS2_KS3_Maths_Guidance_2017.pdf)). Eedi shows the production model: **36,000 distractors mapped to misconceptions across 12,000 questions**, analysed over 4 million responses ([Eedi](https://eedi.substack.com/p/what-do-4-million-students-responses)). Two caveats apply. A distractor choice is probabilistic evidence, not proof. And LLM-generated distractors align poorly with the misconceptions human authors target ([arXiv 2405.05144](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05144v2)), which supports keeping distractors human-authored data. For Barruu's engine this means every choice distractor and every recognised wrong numeric or symbolic answer carries a `misconceptionId`. Feedback is keyed to that id and translated through overlays, and the learner model tracks P(misconception) alongside P(skill).

Many classic misconceptions were **not sourced in this research** and need citations before they are published as claims. They are still sensible authoring targets: −3² vs (−3)²; (a+b)² = a² + b²; √(a+b) = √a + √b; f(x) read as f·x; 2³ = 6; log(a+b) = log a + log b; dividing by x and losing a root; applying the zero-product rule to "= 6"; area–perimeter conflation; judging angle size by arm length; additive reasoning about similarity; the gambler's fallacy; mean vs median under skew; the integral as "only area". The Ryan & Williams and Swan compendia and Eedi's taxonomy are the next sources to pull. One evidence point should shape how Barruu measures this work. Students **preferred** plain problem solving to erroneous examples even though the erroneous examples produced better delayed learning ([DFKI](https://dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/8598)). Error-analysis items should therefore be judged on delayed checks, not on satisfaction or same-session accuracy.

## Lessons should fade from concrete to symbolic and be judged on delayed checks

Barruu's lesson stages (motivate, predict, explore, explain, challenge, practice, reflect, transfer, assess) already match the classroom arc used by IM and Amplify Desmos: warm-up, problem-based activities each with a synthesis, a lesson synthesis, and a roughly 5-minute independent cool-down that doubles as a formative check ([IM typical lesson](https://curriculum.illustrativemathematics.org/HS/teachers/typical_lesson.html)). They also match Japan's hatsumon → kikan-shidō → neriage → matome, in which the whole-class comparison of solutions (neriage) is "the heart" of the lesson ([Takahashi](https://lessonresearch.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Characteristics-of-Japanese-Mathematics-Lessons-2.pdf)). The research says what each stage should do.

**Motivate and predict: try first, for older learners.** A meta-analysis of 53 studies found problem solving before instruction beat instruction-first by **g = 0.36**, with high-fidelity productive-failure designs at g = 0.37–0.58. But the effect reversed for grades 2–5 and for domain-general skills ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/492056)). Barruu should therefore use a genuine "try it before we explain" problem for conceptual lessons from about grade 8 up, and lead with explanation for younger learners and pure skill drills.

**Explore: concreteness fading with specified steps.** The main review recommends moving from concrete to abstract with explicit fading ([Fyfe et al. 2014](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-014-9249-3)). An HCI review of 2,536 papers found the technique "underspecified" in practice ([Suh et al.](https://sanghosuh.github.io/papers/concreteness_idc.pdf)). Lesson data should therefore name each fade step (tiles, then tiles beside symbols, then symbols only), not just carry a label. Singapore's C-P-A and Japan's "observe, manipulate, experiment, then verify" are the same idea at system scale.

**Explain and challenge: worked examples that fade adaptively, then broken ones.** The IES algebra guide's first recommendation is to study solved problems: correct ones first, then incorrect ones, then incomplete ones in independent practice ([WWC Guide 20](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/20)). Worked examples stop helping, and then hurt, as expertise grows (expertise reversal, [Kalyuga 2007 summary](https://my.chartered.college/impact_article/expertise-reversal-effect-and-its-instructional-implications/)). **Adaptive fading driven by a learner model beat fixed fading** in a cognitive tutor ([Salden et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-009-9102-0)). Barruu's BKT estimate should decide whether a learner sees a full worked example, a faded one, or none.

**Reflect: a synthesis for the solo learner.** IM and Desmos rely on teacher-led synthesis, and Amplify's tools exist so teachers can select and sequence student work for discussion ([Amplify digital program](https://rebuild.prod.amplify.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Navigating-the-digital-program.pdf)). A solo app needs a substitute. The best one is a neriage-style screen that shows two or three contrasting solutions, one of them flawed, and asks a menu-based self-explanation ("which rule justifies step 3?"). That stays deterministic and AI-optional. Self-explanation prompts have a small-to-moderate effect across 64 studies ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x)). Comparing methods is also what PISA 2022 found most associated with confidence: students in the top confidence quarter outscored the bottom quarter by **56 points**, and fewer than half of students are regularly given such tasks ([OECD PISA 2022 Vol. V](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2022-results-volume-v_c2e44201-en/full-report/component-13.html)).

**Practice and assess: interleave, space and promote only on cumulative checks.** The strongest single classroom result in this research is Rohrer's cluster RCT in 54 grade-7 classes: interleaved practice scored **61% vs 38% (d = 0.83)** on an unannounced delayed test ([WWC study 88770](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)). Retrieval practice beats restudy and helps secondary students most ([Adesope et al. 2017](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)). Review sets should therefore be arranged so that no two consecutive items share a strategy, and they should recur across sessions. Barruu should copy two production patterns. Like Alcumus, it should mix review of passed topics into new practice and show each skill rating as a transparent "chance you'd solve a typical problem" ([AoPS: Alcumus under the hood](https://blog.artofproblemsolving.com/articles/alcumus-a-peek-under-the-hood-of-our-adaptive-learning-tool)). Like Khan, it should award the top mastery level only on mixed, delayed checks ([Khan mastery](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/115002552631)).

**Hints and feedback: step-level and informative.** Feedback averages **d = 0.48**, with huge variation that depends on how much information it carries ([Wisniewski et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)). Step-based tutors reach about **0.76** against answer-only systems at about **0.31** ([VanLehn 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764)). Hint ladders should be authored data (principle, then partial step, then bottom-out step), and multi-step items should check each step. This is also the honest answer to the most common criticism of Brilliant, that its explanations are shallow ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/brilliant.org?page=9)). After each check, Barruu should give a real worked explanation and offer optional AoPS-depth challenge problems.

**Expect modest effects.** Cognitive Tutor Algebra I, at scale and randomised, showed nothing in year one and about **0.2 SD** in year two ([RAND / Pane et al.](https://www.rand.org/pubs/articles/2013/cognitive-tutor-encouraging-signs-for-computers-in.html)). ITS effects shrink from 0.73 on local tests to **0.13 on standardized tests** ([Kulik & Fletcher, IDA](https://www.ida.org/-/media/feature/publications/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems/d-4664.ashx)). Outcomes also depend on dosage: Khan's correlational data tie 30+ minutes a week to larger gains ([Khan efficacy](https://2023-2024.annualreport.khanacademy.org/efficacy-results)), and Zearn's RCT effect was largest for students who started below proficiency ([Zearn](https://about.zearn.org/press-releases/evidence-for-essa)). Barruu should therefore invest in low-pressure habit features with no daily caps (Brilliant's free-tier cap draws complaints) and in good bridging for learners who place low. Causal evidence for secondary digital maths is thin; no independent efficacy study of Brilliant was found. Publishing anonymised, opt-in pre/post placement data would be a real differentiator.

## A two-stage placement should find the learner's "ready to learn" fringe on a skill graph

Knowledge Space Theory gives Barruu's skill DAG a formal meaning. Prerequisites form a surmise relation, feasible knowledge states are its downward-closed sets, and **"ready to learn" is the outer fringe**: the unmastered skills whose prerequisites are all mastered ([Falmagne & Doignon, arXiv 1511.06757](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06757)). ALEKS shows this works at scale. Its courses have **300–600 granular topics** (e.g. "solving a compound linear inequality"). Prerequisites shrink College Placement's 314 items from 2^314 possible subsets to about 10^23 feasible states, and a 314-item placement places 548,000 students from basic maths to calculus in about 2.1 hours ([ALEKS practical perspective](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf)). ALEKS asks the item whose probability of being known is near 0.5 and updates the probabilities of whole states. It models careless errors explicitly and assumes lucky guesses are rare on open-response items. It validates every test with a randomly chosen "extra problem" whose answer the algorithm ignores ([same source](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-practical-perspective-knowledge-space-theory.pdf)).

The recommended design keeps what Barruu already has and adds graph search. **Stage 1** is the existing Rasch/EAP test, extended below grade 9 to grade 6, which picks a coarse band. **Stage 2** is a KST-style search inside that band. It picks a skill with posterior mastery near 0.5, asks a generated open-response item checked by the engine, and propagates the result: a correct answer raises the probability of the skill's ancestors, and a wrong answer lowers the probability of its descendants. Slip and guess parameters are explicit (lower guess for open response than for multiple choice). The test stops when the state is confident or at a cap of about 20–30 items. That cap is a design choice, not a sourced figure, and calibration should tune it. The output is the outer fringe, which becomes the first recommended lessons and **seeds the BKT priors**. That closes STATE known issue 5. At the precalculus→calculus gateway, Stage 2 must include original covariation items built on the function lab, modelled on the constructs that the PCA and CCR validate ([PhysPort PCA](https://www.physport.org/assessments/PCA)). Procedural items alone miss the documented gap. Barruu should also keep one held-out item per test, logged opt-in and offline, to measure calibration over time.

The graph itself needs four engineering decisions. First, **granularity**: aim for ALEKS-scale nodes (roughly 40–80 skills per course, a few hundred per band), each with authored or generated item families. Second, **seeding**: start from the Achieve the Core Coherence Map, whose `buildsTowards` edges are published as a dataset with REST and MCP access through Learning Commons ([Learning Commons dataset](https://docs.learningcommons.org/knowledge-graph/datasets/math-coherence-map.md)). Its page names no licence, so check that before reusing the data. Re-express it as Barruu's own skills and use CCSS codes only as tags. ALEKS refines expert edges with response data, and Barruu should plan for the same with `DAKS`-style item-tree analysis once data exists ([DAKS](https://anorien.csc.warwick.ac.uk/CRAN/web/packages/DAKS/vignettes/DAKS.pdf)). Third, **alternative routes** (trigonometry via the unit circle *or* via right-triangle similarity) break a pure AND-DAG. Keep the DAG and add an "equivalent path" annotation rather than adopting full surmise systems. Fourth, **unify vocabularies**: lesson `concepts` and curriculum `skills` must become one id space (STATE known issue 4) so that lesson evidence moves the same nodes the placement test reads. Misconceptions should be first-class nodes attached to skills. The existing `conceptGraph.ts` and `recommend.ts` are the natural homes for this.

## Alignment tags let one graph serve five syllabi

Barruu should tag every skill with the codes it satisfies in each framework, and derive "syllabus views" (a GCSE Higher path, an AP Precalculus path, an IB AA SL path) by filtering the graph rather than writing separate courses. The practices also converge. CCSS's eight Mathematical Practices, AP's skill categories, England's "fluency, reasoning, problem solving", A-level's overarching themes (proof, problem solving, modelling) and Singapore's pentagon (which uniquely adds **metacognition and attitudes**) map onto Barruu's stages: predict and explore for problem solving, explain and reflect for reasoning and metacognition, and transfer for modelling ([CCSS Practices](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Practice/); [DfE KS3](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7c1408e5274a1f5cc75a68/SECONDARY_national_curriculum_-_Mathematics.pdf); [MOE syllabus](https://www.math.mun.ca/~hsgaskill/refs/maths-secondary.pdf)). England and Singapore assess problem solving heavily in the exam itself (GCSE Higher AO3 30%; Singapore Additional Maths AO2 50%), so Barruu's challenge and transfer items are not optional decoration.

## Build the grade-8 hinge first, in five phases

The build order follows leverage, meaning how many later skills depend on a piece, combined with reuse of what Barruu already has.

**Phase 0, infrastructure (before new content).** Unify lesson concepts with curriculum skills. Add `misconceptionId` to distractors and recognised wrong answers. Add framework alignment tags with versions. Extend the skill map and placement items down to grade 6. Seed BKT from placement. Key translation overlay paths by step and option id (STATE known issue 9) before multiplying content across languages. Build the **number line** interactive, which is the cheapest high-reach interactive and needs only point and interval checkers.

**Phase 1, the hinge.** Build **H1 Linear relationships and the function idea**, using an upgraded function lab with linked tables and the "drive the graph" covariation mode. It addresses slope–height and graph-as-picture misconceptions and the calculus-readiness gap, and it sits on the grade 8 convergence point. Build **F3 Expressions and equations** alongside it, with the **balance scale → algebra tiles** board. Then build **F1 Numbers on the line** so that learners who place below grade 8 have somewhere to go, using the number line from Phase 0.

**Phase 2, the geometry spine.** Extend *Mathematical Thinking* into F4. Grow the grid board into the **transformation and construction board** for H4 and H5. Build the **vector board** at the same time as physics' queued force/vector diagram (one renderer, two subjects) and ship K4 early, since 2D vectors are the largest gap between US and international scope.

**Phase 3, quadratics and data.** K1 reuses the tiles. The **probability simulator** and data plotter enable F5, K2 and K3.

**Phase 4, advanced and calculus.** V1–V5 come next, using the unit circle and the function lab's secant/tangent and Riemann modes. Then V6 and V7 with the proof builder, then U1–U2. Phase 5 adds the university-year-1 courses (U3–U6) and the "Get ready" bridges generated from the graph.

Each phase should end with a recorded check, consistent with AGENTS.md's "verify, don't claim": content-verifier and pedagogy-reviewer passes, misconception coverage counts, and once learners exist, delayed-retention and held-out-item calibration reports.

## Conclusion

What separates world-class curricula is not better individual lessons. It is coherence: a tight dependency order, a few strands revisited with depth, and formal content introduced early. Barruu already has the machinery that makes coherence measurable: a skill DAG, a BKT learner model, a Rasch placement test and deterministic answer checks. A static textbook cannot do what that machinery allows. Barruu can enforce prerequisites per learner, put interleaved review where the graph says forgetting is costly, and prove with held-out items whether its graph is right. The skill graph is therefore the product's main asset, and the courses are views of it. A competitor can copy a lesson but not a calibrated, multilingual, offline knowledge structure validated against learner data.

The second implication is strategic. The evidence-backed interactive curricula (Zearn, DreamBox, Amplify Desmos) largely stop at grade 8 or Algebra I, and the best interactive secondary content is paywalled or English-first. Calculus readiness, meanwhile, fails on covariation rather than on algebra procedures. A free, offline, multilingual platform that teaches the hinge through linked, manipulable functions, and carries learners to vectors, inference and calculus at international scope, fills a gap the incumbents have left open. Barruu should frame its claims around realistic effects of 0.2–0.4 SD and publish its own evidence.
